The world you already have

States are \((x, y)\) with \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up. Actions are up, down, right, left, in that order. Intended direction succeeds with probability \(p = 0.8\); the two perpendicular directions split the remaining \(0.2\). Bumping a wall keeps you in place. Goal and obstacle cells are sinks: every action stays there and pays 0. The interesting rewards happen on the step into those cells.

Default rewards: \(+100\) for arriving at the goal, \(-1000\) for arriving at an obstacle, \(0\) otherwise. Problem 2C changes the obstacle penalty to \(-500\). Discount on the grid is \(\gamma = 0.8\). Problem 3 uses \(\gamma = 0.95\).

What one episode looks like

Each sample is (state, action, next_state, reward). The reward is for the transition you just took, out of state. generate_episodes stops once next_state is a sink, so the last tuple is the one that collected \(+100\) or \(-1000\).

Do not read mdp.T or mdp.R inside your learners. sample_environment is the only legal way to see a next state and a reward in Problem 2. The fixed policy in Problem 1 is “always right.”

1A · Direct evaluation

First-visit Monte Carlo · 15 points · Lecture 13

direct_evaluation(episodes, gamma) → dict[state, value]

Follow the fixed policy, and for each state write down “what did the rest of this episode end up being worth?” Average those numbers. You never build \(T\) or \(R\).

\[ G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1} \gamma^k r_{t+k} \]

From the visit at time \(t\), add the reward in that tuple, plus \(\gamma\) times the next tuple’s reward, plus \(\gamma^2\) times the one after that, through the end of the episode. Then

\[ \hat V(s) = \text{average of } G_t \text{ over first visits to } s \]

First visit means: inside one episode, the first time \(s\) appears as the state of a tuple, record \(G_t\). If \(s\) shows up again later in that same episode, skip it. The next episode gets a fresh “already seen” set. Every-visit would record every occurrence; that is the other variant, and it is not the one this part asks for.

Compute a return yourself

Episode, always-right, \(\gamma\) on the slider. Rewards sit on the transitions.

Hint 1 — bookkeeping. Keep a running sum and a count per state, not a list of every return. Reset a seen set at the start of each episode. States are tuples, so they can be dictionary keys directly.
Hint 2 — where the return starts. If the visit is at index \(i\), the sum uses episode[i], episode[i+1], ... only. The reward already in episode[i] is multiplied by \(\gamma^0\), not by \(\gamma^i\). Discounting by the index from the start of the episode makes early states look artificially small.
Hint 3 — the shape of the loop. 
sums, counts = {}, {}
for episode in episodes:
    seen = set()
    for i, (s, a, s2, r) in enumerate(episode):
        if s in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(s)
        G = 0
        discount = 1
        for (_, _, _, rk) in episode[i:]:
            G += discount * rk
            discount *= gamma
        sums[s] = sums.get(s, 0) + G
        counts[s] = counts.get(s, 0) + 1
V[s] = sums[s] / counts[s]
Any state you saw only as a next_state never got a sample. Put those in V as 0 as well, including the goal and the obstacle. An episode that starts on a sink still records one transition worth 0, which is the right sample for a terminal state.
Check: a state appears twice in one episode. What do you record?

1B · Sample sensitivity

Written · 10 points · the objection Lecture 13 raises against direct evaluation

Rerun the 1A block at num_episodes of 100, 1,000, 10,000, and 100,000, a few times each. Watch which cells settle and which cells keep jumping.

\[ \mathrm{StdError}(\hat V(s)) \approx \frac{\sigma_s}{\sqrt{n_s}} \]

\(n_s\) is the number of first-visit returns for that state, not the number of episodes. \(\sigma_s\) is large here because a single return is a whole noisy trajectory: one slip into the obstacle contributes \(-1000\), and a slip the other way contributes \(+100\).

Hint 1 — what should move. Cells the always-right policy sits on, especially the right-hand column, get many returns and calm down first. Cells a random start rarely leads through stay noisy much longer. Run-to-run disagreement at 100 episodes, and agreement at 100,000, is the observation the question wants.
Hint 2 — why so many samples. Direct evaluation refuses to use the map. Learning that \((2,1)\) is often one slip away from \(+100\) does nothing for \((1,1)\) until \((1,1)\)’s own episodes finish. Two states that flow into the same successor can have very different estimates for a long time. That is the “B and E both go to C, so how can their values differ?” slide.
Hint 3 — a skeleton you fill with what you saw. Name a cell that was still swinging at 1,000 episodes and say roughly when it settled. Then one sentence on \(\sqrt{n}\): quadrupling that cell’s visits halves the typical error, so decades of episodes buy less than they feel like. Then the structural reason: each sample ignores every other state’s experience, and the returns themselves mix \(+100\) and \(-1000\).

1C · Temporal difference

15 points · Lecture 13

td_learning(episodes, gamma, alpha) → dict[state, value]

Still a fixed policy. The change is that one transition is enough to learn, because the current guess \(V(s')\) stands in for “the rest of the episode.”

\[ \underbrace{r + \gamma V(s')}_{\text{TD target}} \qquad V(s) \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\, V(s) + \alpha \big[ r + \gamma V(s') \big] \]

Same thing, written as a step toward the target: \(V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha \big( r + \gamma V(s') - V(s) \big)\). Use either form. \(\alpha\) is the learning rate, between 0 and 1. A missing state is worth 0.

Update every tuple, including repeats of a state. Do not reset \(V\) between episodes. Do not sum the future rewards yourself: that sum is what \(V(s')\) is approximating. If you both add future rewards and bootstrap \(V(s')\), you double-count.

One forward pass, by hand

Same short episode as 1A: \((1,1) \xrightarrow{0} (2,1) \xrightarrow{100} (2,2)\). Values start at 0. Watch \((1,1)\) on this pass: when it is updated, \(V(2,1)\) is still 0, so the \(+100\) is invisible until a later visit.

Hint 1 — order. Walk the episode from the first tuple to the last. The bootstrap uses the value of \(s'\) as it stands now, which on a forward pass is usually the value learned on earlier episodes, not the return still sitting later in this one.
Hint 2 — terminals. Entering the goal or the obstacle is an ordinary update of the state you left: the reward is \(+100\) or \(-1000\), and \(V(\text{sink})\) is 0 unless some episode started there and taught it something (those samples are also about 0). Then the episode stops. There is no extra update “at” the sink beyond tuples that list the sink as state.
Hint 3 — the loop. 
V = {}
for episode in episodes:
    for (s, a, s2, r) in episode:
        target = r + gamma * V.get(s2, 0.0)
        old = V.get(s, 0.0)
        V[s] = (1 - alpha) * old + alpha * target
After the loops, every state that appeared only as s2 should still be present with value 0. The action is ignored: the policy is already baked into which transitions you were given.
Check: which quantity is the TD target?

1D · Learning rate

Written · 10 points · the exponential moving average slide

Same episodes, \(\alpha \in \{10^{-5}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-1}, 0.5\}\). The update is a weighted average that forgets.

\[ \text{weight of a sample from } k \text{ visits ago} = \alpha (1-\alpha)^k \]

The recent window is about \(1/\alpha\) visits of that state. Half the weight sits on the last \(\ln 2 / \alpha\) visits. Plug in the three regimes the question names:

\(\alpha\)WindowWhat the grid does with \(10^5\) episodes
\(10^{-5}\)~100,000 visits of this cellMost cells are not visited anywhere near that often, so \(V\) barely leaves its start at 0. Compared with \(\alpha = 0.01\), the map looks washed out.
\(0.01\)~100 visitsEnough to average the \(p = 0.8\) slip noise on this 3×3, without throwing away the rest of the data. This is the value the notebook already uses.
\(0.5\)~2 visitsThe last hit dominates. One step into the obstacle (\(-1000\)) swings a neighbor from a sensible number to a huge negative one, and the next ordinary step swings it back.
Hint 1 — say what you saw, then the window. Quote the three regimes with a cell you actually looked at. “Too small underfits, too big overfits” is not yet an answer; the \(1/\alpha\) window is the reason.
Hint 2 — a bigger MDP. The same fixed \(\alpha\) meets each state less often as the grid grows, so the under-update you saw at \(10^{-5}\) shows up at moderate \(\alpha\) too. Lecture 13’s caveat on Q-learning is the same knob: eventually make \(\alpha\) small, but not so fast that late samples never get a vote. Rare states and a non-stationary target (early \(V(s')\) is wrong) are why one \(\alpha\) does not travel.

2A · Q-learning

Random actions · 15 points · Lecture 14

q_learning(num_episodes, mdp, gamma, alpha, max_steps=100) → Q, policy

Values \(V(s)\) cannot pick an action unless you already know \(T\) and \(R\). \(Q(s, a)\) can: the greedy policy is just the best action in that row. Q-learning is the TD update on those pairs, and it is off-policy. The target pretends that next time you will act optimally, no matter how badly you are acting now.

\[ Q(s, a) \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\, Q(s, a) + \alpha \Big[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') \Big] \]
\[ \pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s, a) \]

The starter already zeros \(Q\), loops over episodes, starts each one at random.choice(list(mdp.S)), and stops on a sink or at max_steps. Inside the loop the action is already random.choice(mdp.A). Your job is the sample, the update, the move to \(s'\), and, after all episodes, the policy.

One Q update

Hint 1 — what “max” is not. It is the best action you could take in \(s'\), not the action you will sample on the next iteration, and not the action you just took. Using the next sampled action would be on-policy (SARSA). Q-learning stays off-policy on purpose, which is why random behavior can still learn the optimal policy.
Hint 2 — entering a sink. Call sample_environment, update the pair you just took, then set state = next_state. If that next state is the goal, \(\max_{a'} Q\) is 0 because those entries were initialized to 0 and the loop never acts from a sink. The \(+100\) is still applied to the state you left. The while condition ends the episode on the next check. Do not add another random call.
Hint 3 — the policy, and the autograder’s random stream. Build \(\pi\) once, after learning, not inside the episode loop. 
policy = {
    s: max(mdp.A, key=lambda a: Q[s][a])
    for s in Q
}
max keeps the first best action. On an all-zero row that is up, which is what a sink’s policy should be. Leave the starter’s random.choice lines where they are. An extra random call, or a random.seed inside the function, changes later samples and the numerical test will miss even if the update rule is right.

2B · ε-greedy Q-learning

10 points · Lecture 14, “random actions explore a fixed amount”

q_learning_epsilon_greedy(..., epsilon, max_steps=100) → Q, policy

Copy 2A. The update does not change. Only the choice of action changes.

\[ a = \begin{cases} \text{uniform among all legal actions} & \text{with probability }\varepsilon \\ \arg\max_{a'} Q(s, a') & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

The random branch is allowed to return the greedy action. Do not sample “a random non-greedy action.” At \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) you exploit about half the time, which is why the same number of episodes produces a sharper Q table than pure random actions.

Hint 1 — the two lines the test requires. Every step, draw the coin flip first, and only then maybe draw an action: 
if random.uniform(0, 1) < epsilon:
    action = random.choice(mdp.A)
else:
    action = max(mdp.A, key=lambda a: Q[state][a])
Exploiting must not call random. The rest of the function, including how an episode starts and how Q is updated, matches 2A.
Hint 2 — greedy during learning vs. the returned policy. The else branch is the behavior while learning, and it changes as Q changes. The dictionary you return is the greedy policy of the final Q, built once after the episode loop. Those two argmaxes should use the same tie-break (first action in mdp.A).

Goal \((5,8)\), obstacle reward \(-500\), \(\alpha = 0.01\), 50,000 episodes to start. Sweep \(\varepsilon \in \{0, 0.1, 0.5, 1\}\) and also try fewer or more episodes. \(\varepsilon = 0\) is pure exploitation. \(\varepsilon = 1\) is pure exploration. 3A · A tree where the exploration bonus wins

5 points · Lecture 14, exploration functions · the notebook invites an LLM here

build_exploration_mdp() → (mdp, start)

You are not implementing either agent. The grader trains both, from the same start every episode, for 500 episodes, at most 50 steps, with \(\alpha = 0.1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), \(k = 1\), \(p = 0.8\), \(\gamma = 0.95\). A policy is near-optimal when its true value at the start is at least \(0.9\) times \(V^*(\text{start})\). You need the bonus agent to clear that bar on at least 8 of 10 seeds, and ε-greedy on at most 2.

The bonus, exactly as specified

\[ f(u, n) = u + \frac{k}{n+1} \]
\[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\, Q(s,a) + \alpha \Big[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} f\big(Q(s', a'),\, N(s', a')\big) \Big] \]

The agent always acts by \(\arg\max_a f(Q(s,a), N(s,a))\). \(N(s,a)\) is how many times that pair has been tried. Untried pairs get a finite bonus of \(k\), here \(1\), instead of infinity.

Why this MDP makes the bonus matter

build_graph_mdp turns every edge into an action, at every state. An edge that does not touch the current state leaves you there for sure, with reward 0. An edge that does touch it moves you to the other end with probability \(p\) and leaves you put with probability \(1-p\). The only nonzero reward is \(+100\), received on the transition that arrives at the goal. The goal is a sink. The graph has to be a tree: connected, \(|E| = |S| - 1\), at most 16 nodes. networkx.is_tree is the check.

Until someone reaches the goal, every true reward is 0, so ordinary Q-learning has \(\max_{a'} Q(s', a') = 0\) everywhere. ε-greedy then has nothing to exploit. Its “greedy” action is a random tie, so it is basically jogging in place: from a node of a long path, only one of the many edges actually moves you forward.

The exploration update bootstraps \(f\), not \(Q\). A successor whose actions are still untried has \(\max f = k\). A successor you have already fiddled with has a smaller bonus. So an edge that steps into fresh territory gets a higher target than a no-op, before any real reward. The agent is pulled down an unvisited corridor. Once the \(+100\) shows up it swamps \(k = 1\), the bonus stops mattering, and the greedy policy is just “walk to the goal.”
